Decent News for September

Erik Hermansen August 30, 2025

Why I Don't Want Alexa in My Home

I'm a tech enthusiast. An early adopter. I've been on the bleeding edge of many things. But here and there, I just say "no, not for me" and decline the sparkly new thing.

In the nineties (yeah, I'm old), I thought text messages were pointless. I could instead voice-call if I needed to reach someone right away, or just send an email. Why mess with SMS?

Okay, I was wrong on that one. Later, I came around.

But I was right about NFTs being a mania/grift. The FOMO wore off when I saw cartoon frogs being sold for absurd sums. I'm proud to say I never bought a single non-fungible token.

So sometimes I'm right. And sometimes I'm wrong. But I always try to cut through the hype and decide for myself what devices and software I will use.

And I decided long ago not to use cloud-connected voice assistants. I see them as a potential threat to my privacy.

I love speech recognition and have spent years writing software based on it. I think there is a right way to do it, and a wrong way. The right way is the Decent way. Your data, including the words coming from your mouth, stay on your local device unless you explicitly choose to send that data. (And I don't mean you clicked "accept" on some unread EULA. A Decent App requires the user to take an action to send their data elsewhere.)

The Standard Cloud Privacy Solutions

The big players (Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, etc.) tend to handle privacy around collecting your speech about the same way.

There is a privacy policy. You and I probably don't bother reading it. Sometimes that policy gets updated later to be less protective. The criteria for it being legally binding is nuanced. And this legalese doesn't protect your data from breaches by hackers. (They're criminals. They don't care about privacy policies!)

I don't think that billion-dollar tech companies run rampant. I assume they are rigorous about their privacy policies as far as protecting their financial interests and reputation. But they've all got wiggle room on how they use your data. The transcripts and maybe your recorded speech will be sent to the cloud. The retention time and allowed uses of your retained data varies. And many of the legitimate-sounding uses, e.g. "to improve the quality of our products", are nebulous and could legally be extended to activities we might not like.

Ironically, the very need to reassure us about privacy creates a curious handicap — the wake word.

The Wake Word

When you say "Siri" or "Okay Google" or "Alexa", the assistant "wakes up" and starts "listening". Or rather, the assistant software enters a state where the microphone audio can be captured and sent to the cloud for processing.

I'm not against wake words - there are technical reasons to use them.

It's computationally costly to parse all the audio for your words that may or may not be relevant to the assistant. (In particular, if data must be sent all the way to the cloud.) And having an extra signal from you (the wake word) that you intend to issue a command or query cuts down on the ambiguity of processing speech. When you ask "who let the dogs out?" you might be requesting a novelty pop hit from the 2000s. Or you might be asking your roommates a simple question.

There are also business and legal reasons why wake words exist.

If Amazon bluntly said, "we will listen to everything you say all the time and send it to our data center", only exhibitionists would be excited about buying Alexas. The wake word allows you to divide your speech into "private" (not sent to cloud) and "sorta-private" (sent) categories. This division legitimately gives you more privacy and strengthens assurances made by Amazon and other companies.

So there are solid reasons for using a wake word to designate speech audio for the assistant. But it makes the conversation transactional, unnatural, and less enjoyable. If we aren't burdened with a cloud architecture for processing speech, we can just... talk. No wake words needed.

Continuous Listening

With Decent Apps, nothing is sent to the cloud. This is what allows us to get rid of the wake word.

I've been working on a Decent App called "Bintopia". It listens continuously unless you tell it to "sleep". Bintopia tracks where you put things. You just talk out loud while you're organizing the items of your house. You say things like "I'm adding the sewing needles and t-shirts to bin #7", and it will reply back aloud "sewing needles and t-shirts added".

This is a simple conversation - nothing too impressive. But the lack of wake words really feels right. And it's fast, because there are no server roundtrips to process the speech. There is a screen that shows where your items are. But you can ignore it and just talk  to the app from across the room as you work.

To be fair, there are tradeoffs with local speech recognition compared to more sophisticated solutions used by Alexa and others. Cloud-based models are generally needed to recognize names of products, movies, books, and songs -- what NLP researchers called "named entities". And though good accuracy can be achieved on-device, the best word error rates require server hardware with specs beyond typical consumer devices.

So yeah, cloud-based speech recognition is still better in some ways. But if you focus on providing a fast, responsive, conversational experience, then local is what you need. There's something very pleasant about having the assistant hang out with you in a continuous listening mode. You only get this feeling if you don't need to worry about it spying on or manipulating you.

I love this calm, secure vibe. It's what I want every Decent App to evoke.

Make Your Own Continuous-Listening App

If you're a developer, you can make a voice-enabled Decent App quite easily. See my Bintopia code (https://github.com/erikh2000/bintopia) for an example. This particular project integrates with vosk-browser, a Kaldi-based speech recognition library. You might instead use OpenAI's popular Whisper library, which is also purely local. Or PicoVoice. There are options.

You might be new to Decent Apps in general. Use our free open source tool to create a new app in about 30 seconds. Blissfully short instructions are at https://decentapps.net/info/create-decent-app-quick-start.html

Your app might be an LLM-powered alarm clock that swears at you. Or it could be a bot that laughs at all your jokes. Or a gamemaster that hosts Dungeons and Dragons campaigns.

And you can share your app with the World without fear of LLM token costs. Host it as purely static content from any web hosting provider. If you want to host your Decent App from decentapps.net, we will charge you exactly... nothing.
